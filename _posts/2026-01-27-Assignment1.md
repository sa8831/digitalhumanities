# Introduction

In creating this corpus, I have decided to do a distant reading on vampire-themed texts from Project Gutenberg because I was interested in how authors from different time periods (from 1819 to 1914) portrayed vampires. Unlike mythological creatures like Frankenstein, werewolves, and zombies, vampires are always being redefined in different forms of media and literary works. They can be frightening and decaying, beautiful and seductive, or even not be creatures at all, but metaphors for something else. This made me curious on whether authors from the 1800s even use the word 'vampire' to describe their creatures, or do they build them through atmosphere and implication instead. 

# The Corpus
**Research & Context**


Before I proceed with my analysis, I researched all five texts to get a sense of the authors, the publication context, and the general theme. As I found out, all five texts feature vampires in different ways, which is exactly the reason why they are interesting to compare and why they were chosen for analysis together. In other words, instead of choosing five texts with similar characteristics and telling similar stories with vampires as their central theme, I chose texts that feature vampires in different forms: myth, gothic literature, metaphorical literature, horror stories, and academic writing.


"The Vampyre" was written by John Polidori in 1819 and is considered the first modern vampire tale in English literature. It came out of the famous 1816 Lake Geneva summer house party of Lord Byron and Mary Shelley, where "Frankenstein" was also created. "The Vampyre" tells of Lord Ruthven, a vampire of aristocratic and detached demeanor. It is important for my analysis as it establishes the notion of a vampire as a member of the upper class and refined, as opposed to monstrous and folklore-based.

"Carmilla" by Joseph Sheridan Le Fanu was published in 1872 and is notable for being written more than two decades before "Dracula." Unlike "The Vampyre," it is more focused on atmosphere and character relations. It is also notable for its exploration of female desire and intimacy. The vampire in this text is not always explicitly named, which is already a departure from more obvious depictions of vampires in this time period.
