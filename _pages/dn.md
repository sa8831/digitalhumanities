# Reflection on Digital Literacy: A 20-Year-Old Interactive Media Student's Perspective
## Claude Haiku 4.5

As a twenty-year-old Interactive Media major, I exist at an interesting intersection of digital literacy. I've grown up with technology as a constant presence, yet I'm increasingly aware that digital literacy extends far beyond knowing how to use social media or create content. It encompasses critical thinking, interdisciplinary collaboration, and an evolving understanding of how data shapes our world.

In my daily life, I employ a range of content creation and social interaction strategies that I've developed somewhat organically. I use Adobe Creative Suite—Photoshop, Premiere Pro, and After Effects—to craft visual narratives for projects, but I'm also comfortable with accessibility tools like Figma for collaborative design work. For social interaction and audience building, I navigate platforms strategically: Instagram for visual storytelling, TikTok for experimental short-form content, and Discord for community engagement within creative networks. I've learned that each platform demands different literacy skills. What works as a caption on Twitter requires a completely different approach on LinkedIn, and understanding these nuances has become part of my creative toolkit.

For research and data management, I rely on a combination of tools that mirror my discipline's needs. Notion has become my digital workspace for organizing research, managing projects, and maintaining databases of inspiration and reference materials. I use Google Scholar and databases through my university library for academic research, but I've also discovered tools like Zotero for citation management and collaborative research organization. These platforms represent my attempt to bring structure to the inherent chaos of creative research processes.

What strikes me most, however, is how this course has exposed me to the incredible diversity of digital literacy skills present in a single classroom. My Computer Science peers approach data with algorithmic precision, thinking in terms of computational efficiency and system architecture. My classmates in Film and New Media bring sophisticated visual literacy and narrative sensibilities. The Environmental Studies and Engineering majors introduce domain-specific data visualization and scientific methodologies. Creative Writers and Literature majors contribute critical textual analysis frameworks that prove invaluable when examining digital artifacts. This interdisciplinary environment has fundamentally shifted how I understand my own digital literacy—it's not a monolithic skill set but rather a collaborative ecosystem where different perspectives strengthen the whole.

The digital humanities framework emerging in this course has opened new horizons for my Interactive Media practice. I'm beginning to see how computational thinking—breaking problems into manageable components, recognizing patterns, abstracting principles—applies not just to coding but to creative problem-solving. When I consider how to analyze user behavior, create data visualizations for storytelling, or even structure a complex interactive narrative, I'm employing computational thinking without necessarily writing a single line of code. This realization has been transformative.

Collaboration has proven essential in developing this computational mindset. Working with Computer Science majors on projects has taught me to articulate problems more precisely and think about systems and workflows. Conversely, I've watched my technically-focused peers discover new creative possibilities when exposed to design thinking and narrative frameworks. This cross-pollination accelerates growth in ways that siloed learning never could. We're collectively learning that the future of digital work isn't about individual expertise but about bridge-building between domains.

AI and data manipulation represent both exciting and concerning frontiers in my digital literacy journey. I've experimented with generative AI tools for content creation and brainstorming, but I'm grappling with critical questions about authenticity, attribution, and agency. I'm learning Python basics to understand data manipulation beyond user interfaces, discovering that understanding data structures and transformation processes gives me more control and intentionality in my work. Yet I'm also discovering the limits of my current abilities—I can create with these tools, but I'm only beginning to understand the mechanisms underneath.

What I'm discovering most acutely is that true digital literacy in 2026 requires intellectual humility. The landscape shifts constantly. Technologies emerge and evolve faster than we can master them. Rather than trying to achieve comprehensive expertise, I'm learning to be comfortable with iterative learning, asking good questions, and knowing how to research unfamiliar tools and concepts.

Looking forward, I'm particularly interested in exploring how critical data literacy might inform more ethically-conscious design practices. I want to develop stronger foundational technical skills while maintaining my creative sensibilities. I'm drawn to questions about how we represent complex information accessibly, how we might use computational methods to amplify marginalized voices, and how art and code might collaborate more fundamentally. Most importantly, I want to remain curiously skeptical—embracing new technologies and ways of thinking while maintaining critical perspectives about their implications and limitations.

# Navigating the Digital Current: A Student’s Journey into Interactive Media and Beyond
## Gemini 3 Flash (Preview)

Entering my third year as an Interactive Media major at twenty years old, I often feel like a native citizen of a digital world that is constantly redrawing its borders. My generation is frequently labeled "digitally native," a term that suggests an innate understanding of technology. However, my journey through university has taught me that being a consumer of digital culture is vastly different from being a literate practitioner within it. Digital literacy is not a destination but a process of constant adaptation, critical engagement, and interdisciplinary bridge-building.

In my daily and academic life, I have cultivated a diverse toolkit for content creation and social interaction. For visual storytelling, the Adobe Creative Suite—specifically Photoshop, Premiere Pro, and After Effects—serves as my primary language. These tools allow me to transform abstract ideas into tangible media, yet I am increasingly drawn to the collaborative potential of Figma for UI/UX design. My social interaction strategy is equally multifaceted; I use Discord for real-time collaboration with fellow creators, Instagram for portfolio-building and visual narratives, and TikTok as a laboratory for understanding viral mechanics and algorithmic trends. Each platform requires a distinct "dialect" of digital literacy, moving beyond simple usage to an understanding of how these spaces shape human connection and information flow.

Research and data management have required a more structured approach. I rely heavily on Notion to create customized databases that track my project milestones, inspiration boards, and academic readings. For more rigorous academic research, I navigate Google Scholar and university-specific databases, using Zotero to manage citations and organize the vast amount of PDF data I collect. These tools have helped me move from merely "finding" information to "managing" knowledge, a shift that is central to my development as a student.

One of the most profound aspects of my current coursework is the physical space of the classroom itself—a room filled with students from Computer Science, Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Engineering, and Creative Writing. This interdisciplinary environment has been a catalyst for my growth. I see my Computer Science peers treating data as a logic puzzle, focusing on efficiency and backend resilience. My Engineering and Environmental Studies colleagues approach data as a mirror of the physical world, using it to model complex systems or climate patterns. Meanwhile, the Literature and Creative Writing majors remind us that data is ultimately a human artifact, subject to bias, narrative framing, and historical context. Learning to "speak" across these different disciplinary literacies has been more valuable than mastering any single piece of software.

The Digital Humanities (DH) framework has offered me a new horizon for my studies. In Interactive Media, we often focus on the "how" of creation, but DH asks the "why" and "for whom." It has introduced me to the concept of "computational thinking" as a creative methodology—not just a way to write code, but a way to decompose problems, recognize patterns, and build modular systems. Collaboration has been the primary vehicle for this development. Working alongside a Computer Science major on an interactive exhibit, I realized that my design thinking and their algorithmic thinking were two sides of the same coin. This cross-pollination has pushed me to move beyond the user interface and begin thinking about the data structures and logic that power our digital experiences.

The world of AI and large-scale data manipulation is the current frontier of my curiosity and concern. I am currently learning the basics of Python to move beyond the constraints of "no-code" platforms, realizing that true agency in a digital world requires at least a foundational understanding of the languages that build it. I am exploring how AI can be a "co-pilot" in the creative process—used for brainstorming, rapid prototyping, or cleaning large datasets—while remaining deeply critical of the ethical implications regarding data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the potential for digital misinformation.

Looking honestly at my current abilities, I see a student who is proficient in creation but still developing in deep technical analysis. I am discovering that my "computational thinking" is still in its formative stages; I can conceptualize systems, but I am still learning how to implement them with the precision of my CS peers. In this course, I am discovering that data is not just numbers or pixels—it is a narrative, a tool for power, and a medium for artistic expression.

Moving forward, I want to explore new ways of thinking that bridge the gap between technical rigor and humanistic inquiry. I am interested in how we can use data visualization not just for clarity, but for advocacy—giving voice to marginalized communities through interactive storytelling. I want to continue developing my Python skills to better understand the "black box" of AI, and I hope to find more opportunities for radical collaboration across disciplines. Ultimately, my goal is to remain a lifelong learner who views technological change not with anxiety, but with a critical and creative curiosity.

# Digital Literacy in 2026: A Third Reflection from an Interactive Media Student
## GPT-4.1

As a 20-year-old Interactive Media major, I find myself constantly navigating the evolving landscape of digital literacy. Growing up in a world saturated with technology, I once assumed that being "digitally literate" simply meant knowing how to use the latest apps or edit a video. But university life—and especially this course—has challenged me to rethink what digital literacy truly means. It’s not just about technical proficiency, but about critical engagement, ethical awareness, and the ability to collaborate across disciplines.

In my daily and academic routines, I rely on a blend of creative and analytical tools. For content creation, Adobe Creative Suite is my go-to: Photoshop for image editing, Premiere Pro for video, and After Effects for motion graphics. These platforms allow me to express ideas visually and experiment with storytelling techniques. Figma has become indispensable for collaborative design, especially when working on group projects that require real-time feedback and iteration. I also use Canva for quick social media graphics, and occasionally dabble in Blender for 3D modeling when a project calls for it.

Social interaction in the digital age is both an art and a science. I curate my online presence on Instagram, using it as a portfolio and a way to connect with other creatives. TikTok is my playground for testing out short-form content and understanding what makes something go viral. Discord is where I build and maintain creative communities—whether it’s for class projects, gaming, or sharing resources. Each platform has its own culture and unwritten rules, and part of my digital literacy journey has been learning to adapt my communication style to fit each context. I’ve also learned the importance of digital etiquette and the impact of my digital footprint, especially as I start to think about my professional identity.

When it comes to research and data management, I’ve developed a system that blends structure with flexibility. Notion is my central hub for organizing notes, tracking project progress, and storing inspiration. For academic research, I use Google Scholar and JSTOR, and manage my sources with Zotero. I’ve started using Miro for brainstorming and mapping out complex ideas visually. For data analysis, I’m learning the basics of Python and Excel—skills that are increasingly important as I encounter more data-driven assignments. I’m fascinated by how data can be used to tell stories, reveal patterns, or challenge assumptions, but I’m also aware of the ethical responsibilities that come with handling information.

One of the most eye-opening aspects of this course has been the diversity of skill sets in the classroom. Sitting alongside Computer Science majors, I’m exposed to algorithmic thinking and the logic behind the tools I use every day. Film and New Media students bring a deep understanding of narrative and visual language, while Environmental Studies and Engineering majors introduce me to data visualization and scientific analysis. Literature and Creative Writing students remind me of the power of words and the importance of critical interpretation. This interdisciplinary mix has taught me that digital literacy is not a one-size-fits-all concept—it’s a mosaic of perspectives, each contributing something unique.

The digital humanities framework has been particularly transformative. It’s encouraged me to think about technology not just as a set of tools, but as a cultural force that shapes how we create, communicate, and understand the world. I’m learning to ask deeper questions: Who is included or excluded by a particular technology? How do algorithms reinforce or challenge social norms? How can we use digital tools to amplify marginalized voices or foster empathy? These questions have pushed me to approach my work with greater intentionality and to see myself as both a creator and a critic.

Collaboration has been a recurring theme in my development as a digital thinker. Group projects have forced me to articulate my ideas clearly, listen to feedback, and adapt to different working styles. Working with Computer Science students has demystified coding for me, while my design background has helped others see the value of aesthetics and user experience. I’ve come to appreciate the power of "computational thinking"—breaking down problems, recognizing patterns, and building solutions that can be scaled or adapted. This mindset is valuable not just for programming, but for tackling any complex challenge.

AI and data manipulation are both exciting and daunting frontiers. I’ve experimented with generative AI tools for brainstorming and content creation, but I’m also grappling with questions about authenticity, bias, and the limits of automation. Learning Python has given me a glimpse into how data can be cleaned, analyzed, and visualized, but I know I’ve only scratched the surface. I’m eager to deepen my technical skills, but I also want to remain critical—questioning how these technologies are used and who benefits from them.

Reflecting on my current abilities, I see both strengths and gaps. I’m confident in my creative skills and comfortable navigating a range of digital platforms, but I want to become more fluent in coding and data analysis. I’m discovering that true digital literacy is less about mastering every tool and more about knowing how to learn, adapt, and collaborate. This course has shown me the value of asking good questions, seeking out diverse perspectives, and embracing uncertainty as a space for growth.

Looking ahead, I’m excited to explore new ways of thinking that bridge creativity and computation. I want to experiment with interactive storytelling, use data visualization for advocacy, and contribute to projects that have a positive social impact. Most of all, I want to remain curious, skeptical, and open to change—qualities that I believe are at the heart of digital literacy in 2026 and beyond.
