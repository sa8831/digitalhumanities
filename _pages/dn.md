## Digital Narrative as an Interactive Media major

As an interactive media major at twenty years old, I am familiar with various types of digital tools including p5.js, Arduino, GitHub, Visual Studio Code, JavaScript, Python, C++, CSS, HTML, and Figma when collaborating on design projects. I conduct my research using Google Scholar, university databases, and Zotero to cite my sources. Before this course, I knew how to use them, I knew why I used them, and I did not think much about what was happening underneath. One small but concrete example of this is how I approach writing. I was used to doing all my academic work in Google Docs, which felt like the default for everything. This course introduced me to Markdown, and at first I relied on StackEdit because seeing the rendered preview made the syntax readable to me. Eventually, I learned I could convert Google Docs directly into Markdown, and by the end of the semester I was writing in VSCode and pushing to GitHub without needing a visual aid at all. It is a minor shift but it reflects something larger about how my comfort with the underlying structure of digital tools changed over the course of the semester. The one thing that changed more broadly is my understanding of data itself, and with it, my understanding of what these tools are actually doing.

Prior to this class, I viewed data as something that was structured into spreadsheets, numeric values, and databases. I understood that texts could be analyzed using computers, and that photographs could be used to identify geographic location using geolocation. I used to believe data was also something you collected intentionally and organized deliberately since it had a clear format and a clear purpose. However, the one thing that I did not think of is that photographs, ancient texts, and names of places from a travel account written during the 19th century could all be considered as data, and used to analyze, cluster, map, and read in ways that machines can process. The idea that working with texts that could be fed into a tool that maps every place name mentioned in it, or that a set of paintings could be clustered by visual similarity using Photogrammar without anyone describing what is in them, was not something I had considered before. That realization changed how I think about what counts as material worth analyzing.

When it comes to using images for analysis, I initially thought of them purely as visuals, things that communicate a message to a viewer. I am now aware that a computational process reading the same images would only see texture, color distribution, and edges. A clear example of this was in class, when several pictures of cats were classified by the algorithm as jaguars. I never have thought the cats looked like jaguars since the only difference was the angle of the picture. Although the program saw something specific, we could not fully understand what it saw. This shows how machine vision measures visual similarity, which is a different thing from understanding what an image is about. Berry and Fagerjord describe computational thinking as a way of breaking problems into components and recognizing patterns, and that is exactly what these tools do. The challenge is remembering that pattern recognition and understanding are not the same thing. As Impett and Offert note, when you read a corpus with the help of a neural network, you also have to learn how the network reads.

This semester I discovered Voyant Tools, which applies the idea of distant reading to text analysis. Distant reading is useful for getting a quick overview of a text without reading every word, but what interested me more was what it revealed about patterns that would be invisible otherwise. As someone in Interactive Media, I tend to think about how information is presented and structured visually, and Voyant made me realize that text can be approached the same way but mapped, clustered, and visualized. While that is the case, it can show you how often a word appears but not what that frequency actually means. That interpretation still requires a person. But as a starting point for finding patterns across a large body of text, it changed how I think about what analysis can look like before you even begin close reading. I also learned to use Project Gutenberg as a source of machine-readable texts rather than just a reading platform, which sounds minor but shifted how I think about where material comes from and what format it needs to be in before a tool can do anything with it.

Spatial data was the area I knew least going in. Working with Recogito introduced me to the idea that a map is not just a visual output but a record of decisions. This makes me think a lot about how interfaces present information to users, but I had not considered that the information itself is shaped before it ever reaches a visual form. Every place name I tagged was a choice, and every choice affected what the final map would show. This connects to something broader about how I want to approach design: the decisions that happen before something becomes visible are just as important as the ones that determine how it looks. 

R Markdown on Posit.cloud was the tool I struggled with most. I had no background in it, and unlike JavaScript or Python, R felt harder to reason through at first. What this experience made me realize is that every tool has its own logic, and fluency in one does not transfer automatically to another. While I am used to pick up new softwares quickly, learning on RMarkdown made me realize on learning the difference between wanting to figure something out and actually knowing how to. It also made me think more carefully about what it means to work with data in a structured environment. In design tools there is usually a visual output that tells you immediately whether something is working. In R Markdown, the feedback is an error message, and learning to read those messages and trace them back to their source is its own skill. I got more comfortable with it by the end of the semester, and I found that the process of debugging taught me more about how data is structured than the parts that came easily.

My relationship with AI tools changed over the course of the semester. When I initially worked on the digital literacy narrative at the start of the term, I used AI to generate three versions of the same reflection and treated it as a straightforward exercise. Looking back now, what strikes me is how confident those outputs sounded despite not reflecting anything I had actually done or thought. They were grammatically fine and covered all the right topics, but they were written from a place of assumption rather than experience. None of them reflected what I would actually encounter in the course like the errors on RMarkdown, the geocoding decisions, the jaguar misclassification. Although I still use them regularly for debugging,  I see what they produce more carefully than I did before. A confident-sounding output is not the same as a correct one since it means the model has seen enough examples of how an answer should sound, not that it actually understands the question..


Going into this course I thought digital literacy meant knowing which tools existed and being comfortable using them. I still think that is part of it. But the course added a way of thinking about what can be treated as data, what a tool is actually doing when it processes something, and where the gap is between that and what you thought you were asking it to do. Images, texts, place names, things I would have just consumed before, are now things I think of as material that can be processed, with all the decisions that involves. Every time I chose how to tag a place in Recogito or how to label a folder of images, I was making a structural decision that affected what the tool could do with it. Looking forward, I want to bring this way of thinking into my own practice as an Interactive Media student. I am interested in how data visualization could become part of how I design experiences. Digital literacy, I have come to realize, is less about mastering tools and more about understanding what they can and cannot do, and knowing that the most important decisions often happen before anything becomes visible.
