# Reflection on Digital Literacy: A 20-Year-Old Interactive Media Student's Perspective
## Claude Haiku 4.5

As a twenty-year-old Interactive Media major, I am familiar with the idea of different kinds of digital technology. I grew up surrounded by various digital technology, from apps to electronic devices. However, I've learned later on in university that digital literacy goes beyond social media or creating content. It also involves conceptual and critical thinking, collaboration with diverse communities, and understanding how data influences society.

In my daily life, I employ a range of content creation and social interaction strategies that I've developed somewhat organically. I currently use p5.js, Arduino, Jetbrains, Github, and Visual Studio Code to help build sites and small coding projects and experiments with JavaScript, Python, C++, CSS and HTML. I am open to learning more about Adobe Creative Suite—Photoshop, Premiere Pro, and After Effects—to help craft visual narratives for projects. However, I'm also comfortable with accessibility tools like Figma for collaborative design work. I've learned that each platform demands different literacy skills.

For research and data management, I rely on a combination of tools that mirror my discipline's needs. Notion has become my digital workspace for organizing research, managing projects, and maintaining databases of inspiration and reference materials. I use Google Scholar and databases through my university library for academic research, but I've also discovered tools like Zotero for citation management and collaborative research organization. These platforms represent my attempt to bring structure to the inherent chaos of creative research processes.

What strikes me most, however, is how this major has exposed me to the incredible diversity of digital literacy skills present in a single classroom. In terms of data computer science students usually think about them in terms of how it's processed and optimized My classmates in Film and New Media bring sophisticated visual literacy and narrative sensibilities. The Environmental Studies and Engineering majors introduce domain-specific data visualization and scientific methodologies. Creative Writers and Literature majors contribute critical textual analysis frameworks that prove invaluable when examining digital artifacts. This interdisciplinary environment has fundamentally shifted how I understand my own digital literacy—it's not a monolithic skill set but rather a collaborative ecosystem where different perspectives strengthen the whole.

The digital humanities aspect in this major gave me new perspectives for my Interactive Media practice. I'm beginning to see how computational thinking—breaking problems into manageable components, recognizing patterns, abstracting principles—applies not just to coding but to creative problem-solving. When I consider how to analyze user behavior, create data visualizations for storytelling, or even structure a complex interactive narrative, I'm employing computational thinking without necessarily writing a single line of code. 

Collaboration has proven essential in developing this computational mindset. Working alongside Computer Science majors on projects has taught me to articulate problems more precisely and think about systems and workflows. Conversely, I've watched my technically-focused peers discover new creative possibilities when exposed to design thinking and narrative frameworks. This kind of cross-disciplinary exchange has been valuable. We're collectively learning that the future of digital work isn't about individual expertise but about bridge-building between domains.

AI and data manipulation represent both exciting and concerning frontiers in my digital literacy journey. I've experimented with generative AI tools for content creation and brainstorming, but I'm grappling with critical questions about authenticity, attribution, and agency. I'm exploring  Python basics to understand data manipulation beyond user interfaces, discovering that understanding data structures and transformation processes gives me more control and intentionality in my work. Yet I'm also discovering the limits of my current abilities—I can create with these tools, but I'm only beginning to understand the mechanisms underneath.

What I'm discovering most acutely is that true digital literacy in 2026 requires intellectual humility. The landscape shifts constantly. Technologies emerge and evolve faster than we can master them. Rather than trying to achieve comprehensive expertise, I'm learning to be comfortable with iterative learning, asking good questions, and knowing how to research unfamiliar tools and concepts.

Looking forward, I'm particularly interested in exploring how critical data literacy might inform more ethically-conscious design practices. I want to develop stronger foundational technical skills while maintaining my creative sensibilities. I'm drawn to questions about how we represent complex information accessibly, how we might use computational methods to support underrepresented perspectives, and how art and code might collaborate more fundamentally. Most importantly, I want to remain curiously skeptical—embracing new technologies and ways of thinking while maintaining critical perspectives about their implications and limitations.

# Navigating the Digital Current: A Student’s Journey into Interactive Media and Beyond
## Gemini 3 Flash (Preview)

Entering my second year as an Interactive Media major at twenty years old, I often feel like I am someone who is vastly exposed to many digital technologies. My generation is frequently labeled "digitally native," a term that suggests an innate understanding of technology. However, my journey through university has taught me that being a consumer of digital culture is  different from being a literate practitioner within it. Digital literacy is not a destination but a process of constant adaptation, critical engagement, and interdisciplinary bridge-building.

In my daily and academic life, I have cultivated a diverse toolkit for content creation and social interaction. I currently use p5.js, Arduino, Jetbrains, Github, and Visual Studio Code to help build sites and small coding projects and experiments with JavaScript, Python, C++, CSS and HTML. I am open to learning more about Adobe Creative Suite—Photoshop, Premiere Pro, and After Effects—to help craft visual narratives for projects. My engagement with digital platforms is primarily focused on academic and creative work rather than social media, and even without active participation, this engagement has helped me reflect on how digital spaces influence communication, collaboration, and the presentation of information.

Research and data management have required a more structured approach. I rely heavily on Notion to create customized databases that track my project milestones, inspiration boards, and academic readings. For more rigorous academic research, I navigate Google Scholar and university-specific databases, using Zotero to manage citations and organize the vast amount of PDF data I collect. These tools have helped me move from merely "finding" information to "managing" knowledge, a shift that is central to my development as a student.

One of the most profound aspects of my current coursework is the physical space of the classroom itself—a room filled with students from Computer Science, Film and New Media, Environmental Studies, Engineering, and Creative Writing. This interdisciplinary environment has been a catalyst for my growth. I see my Computer Science peers treating data as a logic puzzle, focusing on efficiency and backend resilience. My Engineering and Environmental Studies colleagues approach data as a mirror of the physical world, using it to model complex systems or climate patterns. Meanwhile, the Literature and Creative Writing majors remind us that data is ultimately a human artifact, subject to bias, narrative framing, and historical context. Learning to "speak" across these different disciplinary literacies has been more valuable than mastering any single piece of software.

The Digital Humanities (DH) framework has offered me a new horizon for my studies. In Interactive Media, we often focus on the "how" of creation, but DH asks the "why" and "for whom." It has introduced me to the concept of "computational thinking" as a creative methodology—not just a way to write code, but a way to decompose problems, recognize patterns, and build modular systems. Collaboration has been the primary vehicle for this development. Working alongside a Computer Science major on an interactive exhibit, I realized that my design thinking and their algorithmic thinking were two sides of the same coin. This cross-pollination has pushed me to move beyond the user interface and begin thinking about the data structures and logic that power our digital experiences.

The world of AI and large-scale data manipulation is the current frontier of my curiosity and concern. I am currently learning the basics of Python to move beyond the constraints of "no-code" platforms, realizing that true agency in a digital world requires at least a foundational understanding of the languages that build it. I am exploring how AI can be a "co-pilot" in the creative process—used for brainstorming, rapid prototyping, or cleaning large datasets—while remaining deeply critical of the ethical implications regarding data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the potential for digital misinformation.

Looking honestly at my current abilities, I see a student who is proficient in creation but still developing in deep technical analysis. I am discovering that my "computational thinking" is still in its formative stages; I can conceptualize systems, but I am still learning how to implement them with the precision of my CS peers. In this course, I am discovering that data is not just numbers or pixels—it is a narrative, a tool for power, and a medium for artistic expression.

Moving forward, I want to explore new ways of thinking that bridge the gap between technical rigor and humanistic inquiry. I am interested in how we can use data visualization not just for clarity, but for advocacy—giving voice to marginalized communities through interactive storytelling. I want to continue developing my Python skills to better understand the "black box" of AI, and I hope to find more opportunities for radical collaboration across disciplines. Ultimately, my goal is to remain a lifelong learner who views technological change not with anxiety, but with a critical and creative curiosity.
